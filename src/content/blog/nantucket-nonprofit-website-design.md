**Short answer:** a Nantucket nonprofit website needs to make the mission obvious in one sentence, make donating take under a minute on a phone, show real impact with real numbers and real photos, list the board and financials so funders can verify you, and be accessible to everyone. It does not need to be expensive. It needs to be clear, fast, and honest.

I have worked with Housing Nantucket and the Artists Association of Nantucket, and I have seen how much of a nonprofit's credibility rides on a site that most boards have not looked at closely in years.

## What funders look for

Before a foundation, a major donor, or a grant committee writes a check, someone opens your website and looks for specific things.

- **A clear mission** stated at the top of the homepage in plain words. Not a paragraph. A sentence.
- **Evidence of impact.** Numbers, stories, photos. Families housed, students served, acres protected, meals delivered. Update them every year.
- **The people.** Board members, staff, and their affiliations. A funder often knows someone on your board and will look for the name.
- **Financial transparency.** Your most recent annual report and Form 990, or a link to them. Hiding these raises questions.
- **Recent activity.** A news page or event listing with dates from this year. A site last updated in 2023 suggests an organization that has stalled.

Every one of these is a page or a section. None of them requires a large budget.

## Donation flow

The donate button should be visible on every page without scrolling, on a phone. That is the whole rule, and most sites break it.

The donation page itself:

- Use a proper payment platform such as Givebutter, Donorbox, or Stripe. Do not ask people to mail a check as the only option.
- Offer preset amounts with a short note on what each one does. "$250 covers a month of utilities for one family."
- Include a monthly giving option. Recurring donors are worth many times a one-time gift.
- Keep the form to the fields you actually need. Name, email, amount, payment. Every extra field loses donors.
- Confirm immediately with a thank-you page and email, and make sure the receipt is tax-compliant.

Test the whole flow from a phone before every campaign. Broken donation pages during the summer gala season are more common than anyone admits.

## Events and volunteers

Summer is when island nonprofits raise most of their money, and the events pages carry the load. Each event needs its own page with the date, location, ticket link, sponsorship levels, and photos from last year. Publish it in the winter, because sponsors are deciding in March.

For volunteers, a short form and a clear list of roles. "We need drivers on Tuesday mornings" gets more sign-ups than "Get involved".

## Accessibility basics

Accessibility matters for everyone and is increasingly expected by funders and required by law for organizations receiving public money. The basics cost nothing extra when the site is built right.

- Text with enough contrast to read.
- Every image with a description for screen readers.
- Forms with proper labels.
- Navigation that works with a keyboard.
- Text that can be resized without breaking the layout.
- Video with captions.

Ask any designer how they handle accessibility. If the answer is blank, keep looking.

## What to skip

- A homepage slideshow that hides the mission for ten seconds.
- A separate "portal" that nobody logs into.
- Autoplay video with sound.
- A blog you will not maintain. A news page updated four times a year beats a blog updated never.

## Keeping it maintained

The most common failure is not the launch. It is the year after, when nobody on staff knows how to update the events page. Build in a simple way to update news, events, and board lists, and write down who is responsible. A site that costs little to maintain is a site that stays current.
